\begin{abstract}
A language model (LM) can usually answer the same question in more than one way, but reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) is indifferent to which correct answer a model produces. A solution will earn the same reward whether it is the thousandth copy of a familiar answer or one the model has never produced before.
Yet, there is potential value in having the model retain multiple correct solutions as it is trained. For instance, multiple modes may give users a choice and provide problem-solving strategies that improve overall model performance.
Here, we measure solution diversity through RLVR training using \mb{}, a set of multi-solution tasks, where the verifier returns both correctness and mode discovered.
We find that RLVR post-training concentrates probability onto fewer correct modes even as accuracy holds or improves, and moreover, that frontier models are already highly concentrated.
We then introduce Re:Max, which uses the verifier to index a memory containing one verified example per discovered mode and rehearses those modes uniformly.
A solution found once is, therefore, practiced as often as one found repeatedly.
Across three model scales, two RL objectives, and harder task constructions, Re:Max improves both how often a policy succeeds and how many different ways it can succeed.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Training and evaluation of reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) algorithms are driven entirely by model accuracy.
However, what makes models useful at deployment depends on having different candidates to choose from, i.e. test-time search and reranking \citep{brown2024monkeys,snell2024testtime,cobbe2021training,wang2023selfconsistency}. This raises a question: as a model gets better at finding a correct answer during RLVR, \textit{what happens to other correct candidates}?

RLVR trains against an executable check \citep{guo2025deepseekr1}, but its binary reward does not distinguish a solution the model has produced a thousand times from one it has never produced before.
Nothing in the objective asks the model to maintain diversity.
Fig.~\ref{fig:story} shows what this can look like on a graph-coloring problem with six valid answers: under Dr.GRPO \citep{liu2025understanding}, the policy becomes more accurate while its correct outputs concentrate onto a single mode.

Mode collapse, or narrowing of the output space, has been observed in alignment \citep{kirk2024understanding,cui2025entropy}, creative generation \citep{doshi2024creative}, brainstorming and ideation \citep{anderson2024homogenization}, public argument \citep{kim2026argument}, and LM-assisted writing \citep{abdulhai2026distort}.
Quantifying this narrowing requires deciding whether two outputs are actually the same answer.
Token-level diversity \citep{li2016diversity,zhu2018texygen} counts two spellings of one solution as variety, while a judge that groups answers by meaning uses a second model to evaluate whether a given response is distinct \citep{farquhar2024detecting,zheng2023judging}.

Here, we investigate the prevalence and impact of mode collapse in problem-solving tasks under RLVR post-training. Our contributions are threefold:

\begingroup
\setlength{\leftmargini}{1.1em}
\begin{compactenum}
    \item \mb{}: We contribute a benchmark for empirically studying mode collapse in tasks allowing for multiple correct solutions, ranging from code evaluation to constrained planning. \mb{} provides both output correctness and maintains a unique identity for each available modal solution to a given problem. The benchmark spans five executable domains, each with five difficulty levels (Sec.~\ref{sec:modebench}).

    \item We conduct comprehensive experiments on \mb{} across 4 open-weight model families and \MDfrontierdeployments{} frontier models.
    Across open-weight models and at 3 model scales, the number of solution modes collapses in every domain under both Dr.GRPO and GRPO.
    Further, in inference, all frontier models we measured reached fewer than $1.5$ effective modes out of up to 355 unique solutions (Sec.~\ref{sec:results-collapse} and App.~\ref{app:hosted-mode-diversity}).

    \item We propose \emph{mode replay}, which stores one verified exemplar per mode and rehearses all modes equally. Added to Dr.GRPO \citep{liu2025understanding}, it raises accuracy and pairwise correct mode diversity across 3 model scales and all 5 \mb{} domains. We outperform 8 other RLVR baselines. Added to MaxRL \citep{tajwar2026maxrl} (which we dub as Re:Max), it adds diversity and accuracy across \mb{} difficulty levels (Sections~\ref{sec:method} and~\ref{sec:results-retention}--\ref{sec:results-levels}).
    % MARWA GOOD IDEA: Get percentage increase in accuracy and percentage increase in PCMD!!!

\end{compactenum}
\endgroup

Overall, our results quantify the severity of mode collapse and suggest that incorporating mode replay as part of RLVR post-training can lead to both increased diversity of solutions and improved model performance.

Building a benchmark to study solution model collapse is challenging for two reasons.
First, each problem must allow for several valid discrete solutions.
Second, given a model's response, we must be able to determine which solution mode was reached. For example, in Fig.~\ref{fig:modebench-examples}D, we show algebra problems in our benchmark. In this example, we show that the same algebraic solution can be reached through two different series of steps.

Ensuring these two properties hold is domain-specific. Fig.~\ref{fig:modebench-examples} illustrates a subset of solution modes across the considered domains; e.g., in \texttt{Countdown} and \texttt{MathIR}, modes mean the model reached the same answer by a different route, whereas in \texttt{Graph} and \texttt{PantryPlan}, modes mean a different valid answer altogether. We provide the number of unique possible solutions per domain in Table~\ref{tab:tasks}. Refer to App.~\ref{app:domain-overview} and App.~\ref{app:prompts} for per-domain rules and prompts for dataset generation.

For each domain, we provide a verifier that evaluates whether a valid solution exists within the model's response, as well as the mode a solution belongs to. This was built by creating in advance a comprehensive set of potential solutions to the problem. Precisely, for a prompt $x$ with specification $s_x$, the validator maps an output $y$ either to failure $\bot$ or to a key $V(y,s_x)\in\mathcal C_x$.
Two accepted outputs are the same mode if and only if their keys agree.
The full set of valid keys $\mathcal C_x$ is never revealed, neither its members nor its size.
A policy, and the memory that stores its successes, only ever sees keys for answers the policy actually produced.

\subsection{Success and verified-mode diversity}
\label{sec:collapse-metrics}

To measure performance on \mb{}, we must consider two distinct properties: how often the output is accurate, and, given that it is right, which of the available right answers has been given.
A benchmark reporting only accuracy cannot capture the failure we study.

The obvious way to measure modal diversity is to count distinct correct answers in $K$ samples. We define $P$ as the chance a sample is correct and $q_c$ be how that correct probability is divided among the valid answers:
% Closes the gap this display opens under the run-in lead line above it; the
% equation's own \abovedisplayskip already supplies that separation.
\vspace{-0.05em}
\begin{equation}
 \underbrace{\Pr[D_K>0]}_{\texttt{pass@}K}=1-(1-P)^K,
 \qquad
 \underbrace{\E[D_K]}_{\texttt{distinct@}K}
 =\sum_c[1-(1-Pq_c)^K],
 \label{eq:main-occupancy}
\end{equation}
\vspace{-1em}

where $D_K$ counts the distinct correct answers. However, both metrics increase with $P$, and thus a policy that is right more often may have higher $\texttt{pass@K}$ and $\texttt{distinct@}K$ while producing less diverse solutions.

We therefore propose an alternative metric, denoted as pairwise correct-mode diversity:
\begin{equation}
 \pmd = 1-\sum_c q_c^2
 = \Pr[Z_1\ne Z_2\mid \text{both responses verified}],
 \label{eq:main-pmd}
\end{equation}
\vspace{-1.2em}

measuring the joint probability of different solution modes given two correct responses. A \pmd{} of zero thus implies every correct answer belongs to the same solution mode, and an even spread over $m$ yields a \pmd{} of $1-1/m$, e.g., if answers spread evenly across two possible solution modes, then the \pmd{} is $1/2$. We note that when infinite solution modes are possible, the maximum \pmd{} converges to 1, and that when the model outputs no correct solutions, we cannot calculate the metric. We only calculate \pmd{} when a model produces a minimum number of correct solutions; App.~\ref{app:registered-endpoints} gives that threshold.

Fig.~\ref{fig:base-levels-all-scales} shows how \pmd{} captures diversity of responses independently of accuracy. GPT-5.6 Sol has near-perfect \texttt{pass@8} for all five tasks considered, yet much weaker models in the Qwen2.5-Instruct family have higher \pmd{}.
For the context of this work, we define a \textit{solution mode collapse} as a decline in \pmd{} during RLVR.

\subsection{Harder tasks with the same notion of a mode}
\label{sec:levels-design}

We construct \mb{} so that tasks can be made more or less challenging, which helps prevent \texttt{pass@8} accuracy saturation. This allows us to explore the impact of problem difficulty on mode collapse.
For each domain, we include 5 independent difficulty levels,  e.g. for our \texttt{Graph} domain we allow more hidden vertices, and for \texttt{Countdown} we give the model more operands. Each difficulty level holds constant (1) our definition of mode, (2) our approximate distribution of modes, (3) our verifier constant, and (4) the sample size of 384 train and 128 test problems.
To ensure (2) holds, and moreover, that the problems we sample are in fact more challenging, we fit each level with consideration to \texttt{pass@1} and \texttt{pass@8} of a reference model, e.g. Level 4 was tuned such that a Qwen2.5-Instruct-7B model has the same \texttt{pass@1} and \texttt{pass@8} scores as a Qwen2.5-Instruct-0.5B on Level 1.

LMs with a higher parameter count generally have higher \texttt{pass@8} and can solve more problems. Yet, in Fig. \ref{fig:levels}, for our harder problems, we find that solution mode diversity declines, even as our underlying mode count per level remains stable. This is most noticeable when frontier models are evaluated. Even though correctness is saturated, we are able to use \pmd{} to isolate solution mode collapse (App.~\ref{app:hosted-mode-diversity}), and this phenomena happens across \mb{}. For example, frontier models answer $\MDfrontierpass$ of questions from Level 1-3 correctly under normalized grading, but their mean \pmd{} is only $\MDfrontierpmdmean$, below the $\MDsmallbestpmd$ of the most diverse sub-2B model, which answers $\MDsmallbestpassall$ of its prompts.

A common fix for solution mode collapse is to increase the sampling budget, as repeated sampling
can substantially increase solution coverage at test time \citep{brown2024monkeys,yue2025rlvrlimit}. But, across \mb{}, we find that more samples do not recover the complete set of modes. For example, all 1,024 GPT-5.6 Sol draws on the 128 Level-3 \texttt{Graph} problems verify, yet eight draws yield just $1.16$ distinct colorings, and $95.1\%$ of correct pairs repeat one (App.~\ref{app:hosted-mode-diversity}). Fig.~\ref{fig:gpt56-sampling-budget} follows \mb{} at three levels out to 512 draws per problem, and the curves flatten far below the modal set: an LM that has settled on two colorings does not find the rest even after being asked five hundred times.

\texttt{Python} is the extreme case. Its problems admit several hundred distinct return vectors on average, and five hundred draws surface between one and four solutions, under two percent of all possible solutions, and under one percent at Levels 2 and 3. Each doubling of the sampling budget buys a fraction of one mode, against gaps of several modes everywhere and several hundred in \texttt{Python}. This problem becomes worse as we increase problem difficulty. All \MDfrontierdeployments{} frontier models tested narrow in \pmd{} from Level 1 to Level 3 while staying near-perfect on correctness (Fig.~\ref{fig:frontier-level-grid}, App.~\ref{app:hosted-mode-diversity}).

We similarly check our local models over larger samplings, and we find quadrupling to $K=32$ draws per problem moves control \pmd{} by at most $.010$, and \texttt{MathIR} at $K=64$ draws is as concentrated as at $K=8$ (Apps.~\ref{app:decoding-objection} and~\ref{app:sampling-budget-ablation}), while disabling reasoning lowers correctness and verified-mode counts in every deployment tested, though its \pmd{} effect differs by deployment (Table~\ref{tab:hosted-reasoning-matched-levels}, App.~\ref{app:hosted-reasoning-control}).

We do, however, note that cheap inference interventions do exist. For example, we find that because different models collapse onto different answers, mixing responses from two independent models covers more verified solution modes than either alone (App.~\ref{app:cross-model-overlap}). Additionally, raising inference temperature gives more solution modes too, but at the cost of model accuracy (Fig.~\ref{fig:gpt56-temperature-curve}).

\subsection{Theoretical intuition for mode collapse}
\label{sec:collapse-mechanism}

Solution mode collapse follows from two properties of the objective:
1) the binary verifier assigns the same reward to every correct answer, so the objective provides no incentive to preserve one correct mode over another; and 2) an on-policy update can reinforce only the modes that appear in its fresh rollout samples, which are drawn according to the policy's current preferences. Together, these properties create a positive feedback loop, where a mode with even a small early advantage is sampled more often, receives more reinforcement, and therefore becomes even more likely to be sampled in subsequent updates. Figure~\ref{fig:replay-bank-balance} illustrates this process.

We formalize this intuition in three theoretical results, developed in detail in the appendices.
First, Theorem~\ref{thm:grpo-collapse} shows that, under an independent-logit parametrization and Euclidean expected-gradient flow, this positive feedback leads to a ``winner-takes-all'' limit: a unique initial leader among correct modes eventually dominates, as \citet{sinha2026expected} and \citet{lochab2026ucpo} also establish.
Second, App.~\ref{app:theory-entropy} shows that this result depends on the interaction between binary rewards and optimization geometry; binary rewards alone do not force this concentration.
Third, Lemma~\ref{lem:sampled-score-bound} characterizes a limitation of standard RLVR; LMs post-trained with RLVR can amplify rare modes when sampled, but both the chance of sampling a mode and its expected absolute logit-gradient contribution vanish as its probability approaches zero. When the applied advantages sum to zero within the group, a mode absent from the current batch has no gradient.

\subsection{Replaying distinct verified successes}
\label{sec:replay}

Our theoretical analysis suggests that, to achieve diversity in inference we need to maintain our solution modes during training.
We address this challenge drawing inspiration from classic RL approaches towards memory management through replay \citep{lin1992selfimproving, DBLP:journals/corr/MnihKSGAWR13, rolnick2019replay}. Once a successful solution mode has been found and stored, replay allows us to continue training on it whether or not the current policy rediscovers it.
Under a fixed-buffer analysis, a constant replay from the existing set of unique found modes keeps every buffered mode above a positive probability floor (Theorem~\ref{thm:replay-retention}).

For each training prompt $x$, we maintain an initially empty, bounded buffer $\mathcal B_x$.
The first time a verified mode appears, we store one exemplar $e_c$ that realizes it (subsequent rediscoveries do not modify the buffer).
A deterministic schedule revisits nonempty buffer throughout training.
For an exemplar of token length $|e_c|$, the replay loss is

\vspace{-2mm}
\begin{equation}
 L_{\mathrm{replay}}(x)=-\frac{1}{|\mathcal B_x|}
 \sum_{c\in\mathcal B_x}\frac{\log\pi_\theta(e_c\mid x)}{|e_c|}.
 \label{eq:lm-verified-replay}
\end{equation}
\vspace{-2mm}

Uniform weighting breaks the link between discovery frequency, how often a mode appears in fresh rollouts, and rehearsal frequency, how often a stored mode is replayed.
Once a mode enters the buffer, it is rehearsed as often as every other retained mode, so frequently sampled modes receive no additional replay.

Adding this loss to the fresh update from standard MaxRL \citep{tajwar2026maxrl} gives our primary method, Re:Max. But, this principle is modular, and can be applied to any RLVR algorithm. For example, we also evaluate with Dr.GRPO \citep{liu2025understanding} resulting in the algorithm, Re:Dr. Solution modes discovered during training do not enter the on-policy reward group, and contribute only through the separate replay loss.
This separation allows one to vary the RLVR objective and the replay mechanism independently. Finally, our replay buffers contain only responses to training prompts; held-out evaluation therefore measures changes in the policy itself rather than retrieval or recitation from the buffer (App.~\ref{app:algorithm}).

Fig.~\ref{fig:verified-support-story} illustrates how replay changes learning dynamics.
In the standard RLVR setting, a policy learns only from modes present in the current sampling, which often leads previously discovered modes to disappear. Re:Max adds an additional incentive for the policy to continuously generate previously found solution modes that yield correct solutions.
We note that this is fundamentally different from approaches that encourage diversity by modifying the on-policy learning signal to favor rare or diverse outcomes
\citep{song2025outcome,li2026setpo}, as we do not use mode identity to modify the task reward or advantage.

    MaxRL learns from the policy's current samples, while replay stores one verified exemplar per discovered mode and revisits those exemplars with equal weight.

\section{Experimental Design}
\label{sec:experiments}
\label{sec:shared-protocol}

In our main experiment, we examine whether replay improves correctness and solution-mode diversity across different RLVR objectives.
We compare the impact of our replay component against both Dr.GRPO \citep{liu2025understanding} and MaxRL \citep{tajwar2026maxrl}.
First, on all five \mb{} Level 1 train/test sets we carry out five seeds of post-training using Qwen2.5-0.5B, Falcon3-1B, and Qwen2.5-3B. We train for eight epochs with $G=16$ new rollouts.
We note that replay does not increase this sampling budget. Instead, it performs an additional training update using at most $M=16$ previously collected exemplars from the replay buffer for each visited prompt (Table~\ref{tab:run-contract}).
Second, we conduct the same experiment on \mb{} Levels 2 and 3 using only a Qwen2.5-0.5B.
Third, we compare our method with other approaches, including frequency-weighted replay, UCPO \citep{lochab2026ucpo}, RLEP \citep{zhang2025rlep}, GAPO \citep{anschel2025groupaware}, SetPO \citep{li2026setpo}, and fixed Semantic-MaxEnt under a specific setting (Qwen2.5-0.5B, five seed, level 1).

Third, we show that replay gains persist across difficulty levels (Section~\ref{sec:results-levels}), KL-chaining, and domain changes.

\textbf{As outputs concentrate, rollouts under RLVR lose training signal.}
Dr.GRPO and MaxRL learn from a rollout group only when it contains a mix of correct and incorrect responses. Thus,
we audit every rollout group during training to track how often they have provided a non-zero learning signal.
We find that Re:Max systematically increases the percentage of rollout groups with a learning signal (App.~\ref{app:degenerate-groups}); e.g., on \texttt{Python}, 18.5\% of groups have successes and failures under replay, compared to only 1.5\% without.

\subsection{Replay improves both accuracy and diversity}
\label{sec:results-retention}

\textbf{Replay improves accuracy and diversity across RLVR algorithms and model scales.}
We add our proposed verified-mode replay mechanism to Dr.GRPO and MaxRL and compare each against its matched no-replay control across five \mb{} domains and three model scales. Across these comparisons, we find that Re:Max and Re:Dr. (ours) shift the outcome toward both higher \texttt{pass@8} and greater solution-mode diversity (Fig.~\ref{fig:maxrl-factorial}; Tab.~\ref{tab:cross-scale-terminal-effects}).

\textbf{Mode diversity comes from retaining modes; balancing makes it stronger.}
A single-slot buffer, rehearsing one verified success per prompt and never seeing a second key, already recovers \MARrecovered{} of Re:Dr's diversity effect (App.~\ref{app:mode-agnostic-replay}). To isolate balancing itself, we compare uniform replay with a frequency-weighted variant that rehearses modes in proportion to their discovery rate. Holding buffer construction, advantage scheduling, and loss update unchanged, uniform weighting preserves greater verified diversity, most clearly in \texttt{Graph}, \texttt{Countdown}, and \texttt{PantryPlan}: in \texttt{Graph} it recovers roughly 0.6 additional modes per prompt while raising \texttt{pass@8} by 6.6 points.
Across domains, these gains come without an aggregate difference in correctness (App.~\ref{app:frequency-replay-progress}, App. Fig.~\ref{fig:replay-key-weighting}).

\textbf{Alternative diversity mechanisms produce isolated rather than consistent gains.}
We compare Re:Max and Re:Dr.(ours) against UCPO \citep{lochab2026ucpo}, RLEP \citep{zhang2025rlep}, GAPO \citep{anschel2025groupaware}, SetPO \citep{li2026setpo}, and fixed Semantic-MaxEnt (Tab.~\ref{tab:direct-comparator-matrix}, App.~\ref{app:ucpo-progress}).
Some of these methods produce substantial gains in individual domains, e.g., GAPO reaches $2.9\times$ its control's effective number of verified modes in \texttt{PantryPlan}, while SetPO raises \texttt{pass@8} by $147\%$ in \texttt{Python}.
Yet these gains are domain-specific, while Re:Max improves both metrics across domains, raising \texttt{pass@8} by $90\%$ relative to Dr.GRPO and reaching $1.9\times$ its effective number of verified modes (App.~\ref{app:theory-kl-measured}).

\subsection{Replay remains effective beyond the base training setting}
\label{sec:results-levels}

\textbf{Replay's gains persist on harder task constructions.}
We repeat the replay-versus-control comparison within each of the first three \mb{} levels, holding the level and RLVR objective fixed. Re:Dr. and Re:Max (ours) improves average \texttt{pass@8} and \pmd{} under both Dr.GRPO and MaxRL at every level (Fig.~\ref{fig:level2-admission}).
At Level 2, for instance, \texttt{Graph} rises from $1.04$ to $1.55$ effective verified modes under Dr.GRPO, and from $1.13$ to $1.48$ under MaxRL. Gains vary across domains, but remain positive in aggregate even at Level 3 (App.~\ref{app:level2-interim}).

\textbf{Replay leaves more usable alternative correct solutions when the task changes.}
We ask whether Re:Max / Re:Dr has a downstream impact on inference. Ideally, replay should make a model more able to give a correct answer when the original most-common answer is no longer available.
To test, we take a portfolio of eight generated answers, remove one option from the task---e.g. an ingredient in \texttt{PantryPlan} or a color in \texttt{Graph}---and re-check under the modified task.
Across all 5 domains, Re:Max / Re:Dr (ours) are more likely to give a valid alternate answer. When no answer is valid, Re:Max / Re:Dr find a new valid answer in fewer additional attempts than RLVR or tuned-temperature sampling (Apps.~\ref{app:withdrawal-recovery},~\ref{app:pantry-adaptation}).

\textbf{Reference KL and replay preserve different sources of diversity.}
One explanation for the diversity gains of Re:Max / Re:Dr (ours) is that the model retains the diversity of its base. We test this by anchoring the policy to its base: we sweep the coefficient of a KL penalty to the initial policy and compare its correctness--diversity curve with Re:Max / Re:Dr (ours).
The anchor is limited by the diversity already present in its reference, whereas Re:Max/ Re:Dr (ours) is limited by the modes its buffer discovers. Averaged over the three domains with a reportable initial \pmd{}, matching diversity with a KL anchor costs model correctness, although in \MDklFreeNames{} the reference is already broad enough for the anchor to win (Fig.~\ref{fig:reference-kl-plane}). We further show analytically that diversity depends on whether all modes were contained within the initial policy or discovered during training (App.~\ref{app:theory-kl}).

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Measuring solution diversity under alignment} RLHF and reasoning RL can reduce output diversity, entropy, and high-budget coverage \citep{kirk2024understanding,dang2025diversity,cui2025entropy,yue2025rlvrlimit}. Measuring this collapse, however, requires defining when two correct answers differ meaningfully.
Semantic clustering infers it from language \citep{farquhar2024detecting}, which is general but depends on a judge.
\mb{} instead defines solution identity through the same executable check that assigns reward, so two outputs differ exactly when they execute differently: a narrower but cheaper measure, shared across training and evaluation. Preserving alternatives matters because sampling-based selection relies on generating multiple viable proposals \citep{brown2024monkeys,snell2024testtime,cobbe2021training,lightman2023lets}.

\textbf{Expanding diversity.}
Outcome bonuses, set prediction, distribution matching, and uniform-correct objectives all reshape the learning signal\citep{song2025outcome,chen2025passktraining,li2025divergence,li2026setpo,li2026distribution,lochab2026ucpo}, while MaxRL combines objectives across sampling budgets \citep{tajwar2026maxrl}.
Yet existing approaches still depend on what the current policy samples---precisely where our analysis finds that solution diversity disappears.
Memory can break this dependence: standard replay~\citep{lin1992selfimproving,rolnick2019replay}, self-imitation learning~\citep{oh2018self}, and RLEP~\citep{zhang2025rlep} all revisit past successes in proportion to how often they occur.
Instead, we homogeneously balance replay across solution modes, an idea related to explicitly preserving behavioral diversity in quality-diversity methods \citep{mouret2015illuminating,ecoffet2021return}.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
\label{sec:discussion}
\label{sec:limitations}



We contribute \mb{}, a benchmark for studying solution mode diversity in RLVR, and Re:Max, a replay-based method that preserves discovered solution modes during RLVR.
Across objectives, model scales, and task difficulties, replay retains solution diversity while improving correctness. Diversity may itself be a target for optimization: future work can move beyond preserving discovered modes towards training models to produce desired distributions over ways to solve a problem.

\textbf{Limitations.} Replay retains only what sampling finds, and mode count says nothing about the ``worth'' of additional solutions. Our equivalences are executable by construction, so future work is needed to evaluate the benefit and feasibility of replay in more open-ended solution spaces.
