\section{Removing the Replay Gradient}
\label{app:replay-ablation}

\noindent\emph{Main-body link.} This appendix supplies the empirical
remove-one control for verified replay that Table~\ref{tab:component-ablations}
lists, and answers the count-based-bonus baseline raised in
Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

Two of this paper's claims meet in one cohort. The first is a component claim:
Section~\ref{sec:replay} argues that verified replay converts a transient
discovery into a persistent constraint, and Table~\ref{tab:component-ablations}
supports that with an analytic argument rather than a measured removal. The
second is a comparison claim: a count-based rare-outcome bonus with no memory
beyond the collection batch is among the simplest alternatives to \xdr{}, and
until now it was untested. A single arm answers both, because removing replay
from \xdr{} \emph{is} that baseline.

\noindent\textbf{The arm.} B3a is the frozen \xdr{} objective with one change:
the replay score derivative is replaced by exact zeros before it reaches the
optimizer. The same verified banks are still accumulated, scheduled in
deterministic global round robin, teacher-forced, and traversed backward, so
generation, scoring, and optimizer work are identical to the treatment and no
compute-matching argument is required. The open-set rare-mode advantage, the
one-time discovery credit, and their self-referenced controllers act exactly as
in \xdr{}. What the arm loses is not the ability to \emph{notice} a rare
verified outcome, only the ability to \emph{act} on one after its collection
batch.

\noindent\textbf{No tuning.} Every coefficient is inherited from the paired
\xdr{} run's own recorded arguments, and the launcher refuses to submit when any
of them disagrees with the frozen doses. We deliberately did not sweep the
semantic coefficient or novelty credit here: tuning would replace the question
``does this component matter'' with ``can this baseline be made to work,'' and
the second question is only worth asking if the first answers yes. A reader who
prefers the tuned reading should treat this arm as a lower bound on what a
dose-optimized count-based baseline could achieve.

\noindent\textbf{Design.} Five domains $\times$ seeds 43--47, trained from the
same initialization on the same data, schedule, and evaluation contract as every
reported arm: 12 passes, 4{,}608 optimizer updates, $G=16$,
$\beta_{\mathrm{KL}}=0$, rollout temperature 1, and the same fixed 128-prompt
evaluation split with greedy decoding plus four deterministic temperature-one
$K=8$ replicates. Each run is pinned to the GPU model that trained its paired
\xdr{} seed, for the reason given in Appendix~\ref{app:decoding}. The protocol,
including the interpretations registered before any result was observed, is
\path{paper/preregistration/e72_b3a_replay_gradient_ablation_05b_20260731.md}.

\noindent\textbf{Integrity.} The arm is defined by a gradient being zero, so
that is checked rather than assumed: every logged update must report
\texttt{replay\_compute\_only} active and an applied replay score gradient of
exactly zero, and discovery credit must be observably nonzero. A run violating
either is discarded rather than reinterpreted.

\noindent\textbf{Result.} Removing the replay gradient removes nearly all of
the retained support. On Graph coloring \xdr{}'s $2.406$ verified modes per
eight-sample draw fall to $.429$ without it, on PantryPlan $2.186$ falls to
$.564$, on Countdown $1.893$ to $.630$, and on MathIR $.926$ to $.550$. In
every case B3a lands beside the matched Dr.GRPO control rather than the
treatment it was derived from, and the paired rows leave no room for a
different reading: \textbf{B3a is below \xdr{} in all twenty paired seed
cells}.

Against the control the arm is not merely smaller but directionless. It gains
$+.104$ on Graph coloring and $+.003$ on Countdown, and \emph{loses} $.116$ on
MathIR and $.070$ on PantryPlan---a band of roughly $\pm.1$ around a control
that is itself collapsed. PantryPlan illustrates why per-seed rows are
reported: B3a exceeds the control on four of its five seeds, yet its mean falls
below because seed 43 is an outlier in both arms ($.289$ against the control's
$1.090$, where that control seed is itself the only PantryPlan baseline run
above $.6$). Count-based discovery credit therefore buys, at most, a little
breadth on its own, and none of what \xdr{} retains.

This is the registered R3 reading in all four completed domains: an open-set
rare-mode advantage that can notice a rare verified outcome, but cannot act on
one after its collection batch, does not preserve support. It is consistent
with Theorem~\ref{thm:xdr-balance}, whose restoring force acts through a bank
that is scheduled and differentiated; B3a keeps the bank, its schedule, and its
teacher-forced scoring, removes only the gradient, and the effect goes with the
gradient.

\noindent\textbf{Scope.} In the domains reported above this converts the
verified-replay row of Table~\ref{tab:component-ablations} from an analytic
argument into a measured, compute-matched removal, and it is the second
terminal causal contrast in this paper alongside the separated-support
actuator. It also answers one of the baseline families named in
Section~\ref{sec:limitations}---count-based outcome credit without
memory---under conditions favourable to that baseline, since B3a inherits the
treatment's own doses and its entire discovery mechanism rather than a
reconstruction of them. What it does not do is close the baseline question:
matched token entropy, ordinary verified-response replay, \texttt{pass@}$K$
objectives, and distribution matching remain untested, and this arm speaks only
to decoding-invariant training behaviour, not to any of those.
