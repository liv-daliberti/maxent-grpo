ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE AND SOCIETY

Can your AI find another way?

When several answers are right, why does AI keep giving us the same one?

Play all five games

“It is better to solve one problem five different ways, than to solve five different problems one way.”

— George Pólya, as quoted by Marvin Minsky

Your AI agent gets a math question right. Great. Now ask it to solve the problem another way. If the AI system returns the same approach, how much does that help you explore possibilities?

For a tool meant to help us explore, providing one correct response may not be sufficient. For example, a student may need another explanation, a programmer might want an alternative implementation, or an architect may need other designs that satisfy the same constraints. We need AI systems that can help us find alternatives, not just a single right way.

Our puzzles separate two questions: (1) How often is the model able to answer correctly? (2) How many different correct solutions can the AI produce?

“Variety’s the very spice of life,
That gives it all its flavor.”

— William Cowper, The Task, Book II (1785)

This is the problem of mode collapse: when an AI’s outputs become concentrated on a few solutions. Here, a “mode” means a distinct answer or approach, defined by the task. The illustrative graph below shows two AIs with 90% accuracy. One spreads correct answers evenly across A–F; the other mostly repeats A.

We found this pattern across all seven frontier AIs we tested, spanning GPT, Claude, Grok, Kimi and DeepSeek. In our recent study, we had AIs play versions of the same games you just played. Across these AIs, correct answers clustered around a few solutions.

For example, we asked OpenAI’s GPT-5.6 Sol to solve 128 graph-coloring problems eight times each. All 1,024 answers were correct. But each problem had 4–12 valid colorings, and the AI found only 1.16 distinct solutions per problem on average. When we compared pairs of answers to the same problem, 95.1% repeated a coloring.

Earlier research on training with human feedback found reduced output diversity on summarization and instruction-following tasks. A study of AI-assisted writing found a related narrowing: AI edits pushed diverse human essays toward a more uniform style and meaning. Those measurements differ from counting verified solutions, but they reinforce a broader point: improvement on one dimension can hide deterioration on another. Our puzzles let us quantify this loss of variety by measuring both accuracy and the number of distinct solution modes.

Why does this happen? One explanation is how the AI learns. An AI generates answers; an automatic checker determines whether the answer satisfies the task; training uses those scores to update the AI. For a simple correctness reward, every valid coloring earns the same score. The checker gives no extra credit for a solution the AI rarely produces.

The checker’s indifference to solution diversity matters because the AI learns from the answers it generates. As Figure 4 shows, a solution that is already common, or gains an early lead during training, gets more opportunities to be reinforced. That advantage can compound until one solution dominates, while other correct solutions become increasingly unlikely to appear.

Would asking more times solve the problem? Sometimes. But extra attempts draw from the AI’s existing preferences. If one alternative has a one-in-a-thousand chance on each independent attempt, even 100 attempts give only about a 9.5% chance of seeing it. An answer can remain possible while being practically hard to obtain.

Our study asks whether we can prevent mode collapse. We tested a simple idea: when an AI discovers a different way to solve a problem, can we make sure that solution remains part of the AI’s training data? We call this approach mode replay. It stores one verified example of each discovered solution mode and practices the stored modes equally. A rare discovery then gets repeated practice. This improved accuracy and diversity in our experiments, although it cannot preserve an alternative the AI has never discovered, and identifying modes is harder in open-ended tasks.

For students, different correct approaches can expose why a method works. For programmers, alternative implementations can make trade-offs in speed, memory, and readability easier to compare. For architects, multiple workable designs can provide room to adapt when budgets, materials, or site constraints change. In each case, the value lies in having meaningful options to examine.

A tool that offers alternatives gives users more room to exercise judgment. Accuracy remains essential. But when we ask AI to help us explore, we should also measure which possibilities its answers leave out.
